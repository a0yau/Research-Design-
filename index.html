Did the Quota Move the Needle?

Women MPs and legislative initiation in Kazakhstan's Mazhilis

Research Design · smthelse2

Research question

Did the quota affect women MPs' legislative output?

Did the 30% combined gender-youth quota (introduced 2021) affect the average number of laws initiated per woman MP in Kazakhstan's Mazhilis, comparing the 2016–2021 session (pre-quota) to the 2021–2023 session (7th convocation, post-quota)?

✓ Passes all three tests: empirical · scoped · answerable
Why here, why now

Why I think it's worth asking

  • Combined gender-youth-disability quotas are spreading outside Western democracies, but are rarely studied there.
  • Kazakhstan's quota doesn't guarantee more women — parties can meet the 30% threshold using youth or disability candidates alone.
  • Whether representation gains translate into legislative behavior — not just seats — remains untested here.
Epistemology · positioning

Positivist tradition

Positivists treat the social world as measurable and look for patterns across cases (Farrow et al., 2020).

  • My question is cause and effect, with a stated direction.
  • I compare women MPs' legislative activity across a defined pre/post-quota window.
  • The design is deductive: theory → hypothesis → test.
Literature review

Three camps, one open debate

Existing research on gender quotas splits into three positions on what happens to women's legislative behavior after a quota is introduced — and none has been tested on Kazakhstan's Mazhilis.

Literature · the debate

Three camps

CAMP 1

Performance

Quotas increase an individual woman MP's legislative effort and bill initiation through heightened monitoring, voter expectations, and performance pressure.

Jensen & Wang (2025)
CAMP 2

Institutional Ceiling

Quotas expand lower-barrier activities like parliamentary questions, but hit a hard ceiling at formal bill initiation due to committee-level gatekeeping.

Ajwang (2026)
CAMP 3

Regime Control

In electoral authoritarian systems, party leadership controls both access to parliament and subsequent legislative behavior — any shift reflects party strategy, not individual MP agency.

extending Shalaby & Wängnerud (2024); Nurbayev et al. (2024); Kagazbaeva et al. (2025)
Literature · methodological framing

How I'll test between them

Childs & Krook (2008) Substantive representation requires moving past aggregate seat percentages to track individual "critical acts" — such as bill sponsorship — rather than assuming numbers alone produce change.

Testing between these camps means operationalizing that shift: tracking individual bill sponsorship, not just counting seats. That is the empirical approach this project adopts.

Literature · what my design can test

Two camps are testable. One is a lens.

My bill-count design can directly distinguish Camp 1 (increase) from Camp 2 (flat / no change) — those predict different directions.

Camp 3 is different Because raw bill-count data cannot distinguish self-directed initiative from top-down party assignment, Camp 3 is applied as an interpretive lens, not a falsifiable hypothesis — it is compatible with any directional result.
Literature · the gap

What nobody has tested — and what this adds

◐
Data-gap

Individual-level bill authorship in the Mazhilis has never been catalogued.

◑
Test-gap

Because that data doesn't exist, no one has resolved the Camp 1 vs. Camp 2 debate for Kazakhstan's combined quota — or for any Central Asian legislature.

●
This study adds

The first individual-level bill-initiation dataset for Mazhilis women MPs, used to directly test the Performance vs. Institutional Ceiling debate outside a Western or MENA context.

Institutional context

Party gatekeeping, not voter choice

Top-down institutional gatekeeping — not voter selection — determines women's access to parliament in electoral authoritarian contexts (Shalaby & Wängnerud, 2024; Nurbayev et al., 2024; Kagazbaeva et al., 2025).

Voters choose a candidate
✕
Voters choose a party list
→
Party leadership assigns seats

Kazakhstan reinforces this through closed-list proportional representation (Constitutional Law of RK "On Elections," 1995) — party leadership, not the electorate, is the real gatekeeper.

Theory

Theory first, then test

Introducing a legislative gender quota increases the substantive legislative output of women MPs, because structural inclusion lowers barriers to agenda-setting — giving women both the critical mass and institutional legitimacy needed to sponsor legislation.

The mechanism · causal chain

How the quota is supposed to work

Quota raises women's seat share
→
Less tokenism, more networks
→
Institutional leverage
→
More bills initiated per MP

Step 1 is verified, not assumed: seat share rose 27.1% → 27.4% in the 7th convocation (2021–2023), the window this study uses.

Scope

Why the comparison stops at 2023

The 8th convocation (2023–present) is excluded because women's seat share fell to 17.6% — below its pre-quota level. The quota's core mechanism (raising women's presence) did not hold for this period, so including it would conflate two different empirical situations.

H1: The implementation of Kazakhstan's 30% combined gender-youth quota (2021) increased the average number of laws initiated per woman MP in the Mazhilis, compared to the pre-quota period (2016–2021).
